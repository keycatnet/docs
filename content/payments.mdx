# How customers pay

The customer opens the charge's hosted page and chooses **Pay with card** or
**Pay with crypto**. Either way you receive exactly the invoice amount as USDC
on Base, and the customer sees the full price before paying.

## By card

The customer pays with a debit or credit card, or Apple Pay or Google Pay where
the provider supports it. They never see a wallet, an address, or a token.

1. Checkout asks several card providers at once for a price: Stripe, Transak,
   Rampnow, and Coinbase. Each is asked to deliver exactly the amount the
   payment needs, and any provider that cannot is left out.
2. The customer picks a provider from the list, each shown with its total
   price, and continues to that provider's page to pay.
3. The provider delivers USDC on Base, and Keycat settles it to your account
   the same way as any other payment. The checkout page turns into the receipt
   when it arrives.

The card provider is the customer's merchant for the card transaction, so it
runs its own identity checks, usually once, on the customer's first purchase.
Neither you nor Keycat has an account with any provider, and you never collect
the customer's card or identity details. Which providers appear depends on the
customer's country and the amount. Coinbase asks the customer to sign in with a
Coinbase account, so it is listed last.

## With crypto

The customer picks what they hold: a coin and the network it is on. Checkout
offers EVM networks and Bitcoin, with dollar coins first, and opens on USDC on
Base, which needs no conversion. It shows the exact amount to send, fees
included, and a deposit address for this payment. The customer sends from any
wallet or exchange; nothing needs connecting or signing in.

Keycat routes the payment through [Relay](https://relay.link), which converts
it and delivers exactly the invoice amount as USDC on Base. The amount shown is
rounded up to a cent's precision in the payer's coin, so a wallet that trims
decimals still sends enough. A route whose cost would be more than a tenth of
the payment is not offered; for small payments that usually rules out
Ethereum mainnet. [Payment routing and settlement](/protocol/settlement)
has the full quote and every rule behind it.

## Settlement

USDC arrives in your business account on Base. Most payments settle within a
minute; a payment from an exchange takes as long as that exchange's withdrawal.

A payment on a link that names a [payout group](/account#payout-groups)
settles instead to that group's split, which pays each payee and your business
their shares, usually a few seconds later and within a minute at most. You
never hold the payees' money.

Keycat watches every payment two ways: Relay's signed notification, and its
own polling of the payment's status. Neither depends on the other, so a missed
notification only delays a status update.

## Fees

You always receive the full invoice amount. Fees are added to what the customer
sends:

| | Payer pays |
| --- | --- |
| Keycat fee, checkout and fixed-price links | 1% of the amount, at least $0.025 |
| Keycat fee, amounts the payer chooses on your payment page | none |
| Network and routing costs | the actual cost, shown in the quote |
| Card provider fee, card payments only | set by the provider, included in the price on the provider list |

The quote at checkout is the whole price: what the customer sends, or what
their card is charged, is exactly what it says. Card payments cost the payer
more than crypto, because the card provider adds its own fee.

## Reversals

A payment that settled can, rarely, be undone when Base reorganises. Keycat
marks the charge `reversed` and sends
[`charge.reversed`](/webhooks#chargereversed). A transfer that has
settled to your account on Base cannot be pulled back, card payments included:
a card dispute is between the customer and the card provider that sold them the
USDC.

## Where money goes back to

Before checkout shows the address to pay, it asks the customer where they are
paying from. Paying from a wallet app, anything sent back goes to the address
that sent it. Paying from an exchange account, the customer enters their own
deposit address there, because an exchange sends from a shared wallet and money
returned to it never reaches them.

That answer is used twice. A payment that arrives short, late, or in the wrong
coin or network goes straight back there, and checkout says so, with the
amount and the transaction. Nothing short is passed to you as a partial
payment. And a refund you send later goes there too. See [Refunds](/refunds).

A card payment that cannot be completed after the provider delivered it lands
in your account, and is recorded as a payment of what arrived.
